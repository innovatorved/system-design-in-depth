Design: a video platform

Intuition first

A video platform works like a publishing house for films. The author delivers one enormous master copy (the upload). The press prints it in many sizes, from pocket paperback to large print (transcoding to a bitrate ladder), and cuts each copy into short chapters (segments). The copies are stocked in bookshops in every town (CDN edges). The reader's e-reader fetches the next chapter in whichever size the connection can handle right now (adaptive bitrate).

Mental model: upload once, process offline into many small immutable files, and serve those files from caches close to viewers. Almost all the cost is in egress bandwidth and encoding compute, not in the web servers.

  • Trap: confusing containers with codecs. MP4 and WebM are containers. H.264, HEVC, VP9 and AV1 are codecs. The ladder is defined by codec, resolution and bitrate.
  • Trap: transcoding a whole 2-hour file on one machine. Split it into chunks aligned to GOP boundaries (the points where each chunk can start decoding independently), encode the chunks in parallel, then stitch them together. Turnaround drops from hours to minutes.
  • Trap: updating a view counter on every play. Aggregate views in a stream and apply them to the counter in batches. Also filter out bot and replay traffic before the view counts.

Designing a Video Streaming Platform

A video platform like YouTube or Netflix turns an uploaded file into media that viewers can play on different devices and varying network connections. Uploading is only the first step: the service must prepare suitable versions, make them discoverable, and keep delivering the next segment while playback continues.

The scale is staggering. YouTube processes hundreds of hours of video uploads every minute and serves billions of views daily. Netflix accounts for a massive percentage of the internet's downstream traffic.

The Upload Pipeline

When a creator uploads a video, we don't just store the raw file and serve it. The upload goes to an object storage service like Amazon S3.

Because video files are large, we use Multipart Uploads. The client breaks the file into smaller chunks and uploads them in parallel. If a chunk fails, only that chunk is retried, rather than restarting a 5GB upload from scratch. To do this securely directly to object storage, the backend generates Pre-signed URLs for each chunk.

Transcoding and Adaptive Bitrate Streaming

Raw video is huge and not universally playable. The platform must transcode the video into a ladder of codecs (H.264, VP9, AV1), resolutions (1080p, 720p, 480p) and bitrates, packaged in containers such as MP4/CMAF or WebM, to support everything from high-end 4K TVs to 10-year-old smartphones on 3G connections.

Modern streaming uses protocols like HLS (HTTP Live Streaming) or DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP). These protocols break the transcoded video into short segments (typically 2 to 6 seconds; Apple's HLS authoring spec recommends 6 seconds). The video player on the client's device downloads a manifest file listing all segments and quality levels.

The magic is Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR). If the user's network speed drops, the video player automatically requests the next 2-second segment in 480p instead of 1080p, preventing playback buffering.

Delivering Content (CDN)

Serving video directly from centralized application servers would melt the servers and result in terrible latency for users far away. Video platforms rely heavily on Content Delivery Networks (CDNs).

When a user in Tokyo clicks play, they stream the video chunks from an edge server in Tokyo, not from the primary data center in Virginia. CDNs cache the static video segments globally. Netflix takes this a step further with Open Connect, placing custom cache appliances directly inside the data centers of Internet Service Providers (ISPs).

Metadata and Search

While the heavy video files live on CDNs and object storage, the video metadata (title, description, tags, view count) lives in a database (like MySQL or Cassandra). A search cluster (like Elasticsearch) indexes this metadata to power the search bar.

View counting is notoriously difficult at scale. Updating a database row every time someone watches a video would cause massive write contention. Instead, platforms use distributed counters or stream processing (like Kafka + Flink) to aggregate views asynchronously and write them to the database periodically.

Netflix's per-title and per-shot encoding tunes the ladder to each piece of content. A cartoon needs far fewer bits than a grainy action film at the same quality, and AV1 needs roughly 30 to 50% fewer bits than H.264 at similar quality, at a much higher encoding cost.

Worked example: storage and egress

  • YouTube has said that more than 500 hours of video are uploaded every minute, which is about 720,000 hours per day.
  • If every upload got the full ladder above (about 10 GB per hour across renditions), that would be about 7 PB per day. Real platforms encode only low renditions for rarely watched videos, and add high and 4K renditions once a video becomes popular.
  • Egress dominates cost. 10 million concurrent viewers at an average of 3 Mbps is 30 Tbps. That is why Netflix places Open Connect appliances inside ISPs, and why a CDN hit ratio of 95% or more is essential.

Adaptive bitrate recall
From: Designing a Video Streaming Platform

A viewer watching a 1080p HLS stream moves into weak coverage and throughput drops sharply. What does adaptive bitrate streaming do?

Every rendition is pre-encoded and segmented, and the manifest lists them, so the player simply requests its next few seconds at a lower bitrate. Nothing is transcoded on demand; that work happened once, offline, after upload.
Egress and CDN hit ratio apply
From: Worked example: storage and egress

A live event has 4 million concurrent viewers at an average of 5 Mbps, and the CDN hit ratio is 95%. How much bandwidth does the origin serve?

Total demand is 4,000,000 x 5 Mbps = 20 Tbps, and the 5% of requests that miss the CDN reach the origin, about 1 Tbps. 19 Tbps is what the edges serve, not the origin, which shows why hit ratio is the lever that matters.
Segments for live sports staff
From: Segment length trade-off

A sports streamer using 6-second segments hears that viewers see goals 20 or more seconds after TV viewers and that quality adapts slowly. What change fits, and at what cost?

Shorter segments, or LL-HLS/DASH partial segments, cut glass-to-glass latency and let the player switch renditions sooner, but they add requests, manifest entries and CDN complexity. A better codec saves bandwidth but does nothing for latency, which is governed by segment duration.

Key Takeaways

Use multipart uploads and pre-signed URLs to reliably move large files directly to object storage.
Transcode video into multiple formats and resolutions to support all devices.
HLS and DASH protocols segment video and enable Adaptive Bitrate Streaming to prevent buffering.
Rely on CDNs to cache and deliver heavy video segments close to the user.
Handle view counting asynchronously using event streams and aggregation to avoid database lock contention.
